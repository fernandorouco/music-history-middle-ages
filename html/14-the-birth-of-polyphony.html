1.4. The birth of polyphony

Polyphonic secular music

As we have explained previously, monophony was the predominant musical texture. However, just as architecture evolved from the Romanesque to the Gothic style, music moved from monophony to polyphony.
Capilla románica de La Soledad, Palazuelos (Guadalajara). Catedral gótica de Santa María, Burgos.



Polyphony is a musical texture that involves composing music for several voices that sound simultaneously.


We can divide medieval polyphonic music into two periods:

1. Ars Antiqua: The earliest religious polyphony (9th to 13th centuries)

The earliest polyphonic works did not emerge until the 9th century, and they developed from Gregorian chant. Taking Gregorian melodies as their basis, other voices were added at intervals of a fourth or a fifth, since, according to medieval thinking, these were the intervals that sounded harmonious. These early polyphonic works were known as organum.

In the 12th and 13th centuries, an important musical school developed around Notre Dame Cathedral in Paris. Léonin and Perotin stood out as composers of polyphony within this school. These composers developed a variant of the organum known as melismatic organum.

During the 13th century, new and more complex types of polyphonic composition emerged, such as the conductus and the motet.

  • The conductus is a polyphonic composition based on newly created melodies (rather than Gregorian chants, as in the organum).
  • Motets (mot means ‘word’ in French) emerged as compositions for several voices. In the earliest medieval motets, each voice had its own text, sometimes in different languages.

2. The Ars Nova: secular polyphony (14th century)

In the 14th century, polyphony continued to develop through composers such as Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut. Secular music began to take on greater importance, and secular polyphonic genres, such as the chanson, emerged.
